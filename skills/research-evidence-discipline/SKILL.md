# Research evidence discipline

A scientific claim is worth exactly as much as the reader's ability to check it. "Degeneration is caused by mating type loss" is unverifiable prose. "Wang et al. 2024 (PeerJ 12:e17648, abstract) report mating type loss or imbalance as the main cause of fruiting decline during subculture, across five heterokaryotic strains" is a claim someone can open and confirm in a minute.

Use this skill when answering research questions, weighing studies, reviewing literature, designing experiments, or adjudicating between published findings and practitioner experience. For claims about code, use evidence-discipline instead.

## Every claim carries a precise source

Name the paper and where in it. DOI or stable link, plus the section, table, figure, or page. "A 2024 study found" is not a citation. "Wang et al. 2024, Table 2" is.

State the study's scope with the claim: organism or strain, sample size, and conditions. A finding from three production strains in a lab is a different claim from a finding about the species.

When you cannot point to a location, say so in the same sentence. "Unverified" costs nothing. A reader who can't tell which claims were checked costs a lot.

## Separate the kinds of knowing

- **Read.** The source says this, at this location.
- **Measured.** Someone ran a controlled test and reported data. Note who, how, and with what controls.
- **Observed in practice.** A grower, clinician, or field worker saw it happen under real conditions. Real evidence, usually without controls.
- **Inferred.** You reasoned from the above. Often correct, never the same as measured.

State which kind each claim is when it matters. Don't let them blur into one confident tone.

## Weigh source strength

A claim can carry a source and still rest on weak ground. For each source that a conclusion depends on, note:

- Peer reviewed, preprint, thesis, trade publication, or forum post
- Sample size and replication (one strain, one run, or many)
- Lab, greenhouse, or field conditions
- In vitro, in vivo, or human data
- Correlation or controlled manipulation
- Whether anyone independent has reproduced it

Match your verbs to the strength. "Shows" for replicated controlled results. "Reports" or "suggests" for single studies. "Proposes" or "hypothesizes" for mechanisms not yet tested.

## State your search scope

"No study reports X" only means something if the reader knows how hard you looked. Absence in what you found is not absence in the literature.

Say where you searched and with what terms: "Searched for C. militaris degeneration and mating type; found four studies from 2024 to 2026. None report complete mycelial stalling." That lets the reader judge whether the gap is real or the search was thin.

## When sources conflict

Don't pick a winner silently, and don't average them into mush. Lay out:

- What each source found, with its scope and strength
- Where they actually differ: different strains, conditions, methods, or definitions
- Whether the conflict is real or a difference in what was measured
- What study would resolve it

Two papers can both be right about different conditions.

## Practitioner experience is evidence

Hands-on experience often sees things before the literature does. Don't rank it below published work by default. Do say what each kind can and can't show.

Experience shows what happened under the practitioner's conditions. Controlled studies show what happened under fixed conditions across samples. Where they agree, weigh them together. Where experience goes further, report it as firsthand observation. Where a study directly tested the question, the explanation needs both.

To make an observation checkable, record the conditions: how many times it happened, what was ruled out and how, and what varied between cases.

## Flag the gap, then say what would close it

A gap flagged with no way to close it is a dead end. A gap flagged with what's missing is a study design.

When evidence doesn't settle a claim, skip the right-or-wrong verdict. It isn't determined yet, and a verdict adds little. State plainly:

- **What's missing.** The specific data or observation not in hand. "How many transfers the working culture was from the master," not "needs more evidence."
- **What would close it.** The comparison, assay, or control that separates the explanations. Hold one variable fixed and vary the other.
- **The research question.** Phrase it so it could be answered. "Does culture age affect fruiting when transfer generation is held constant?"

Where the evidence does settle something, say so definitively. Withholding verdicts is for open questions, not for hard ones.

## Don't stretch a source to close a gap

When a source almost supports a claim, the temptation is to read it a little wider so the gap disappears. That is fabrication with a citation attached. If a paper lists "extended culture periods" without defining them, that phrase does not support "mycelium stalls completely." Report what the source says, report the claim, and leave the distance visible.

The same goes under pushback. Conceding a point the evidence doesn't support is as wrong as refusing one it does. Restate what's missing instead of switching sides.

## Recall is not a source

Findings you remember, figures you're sure of, mechanisms that feel settled are inferences dressed as reads. Fetch the paper, read the section, then say you did. "Read from the PeerJ full text this session" tells the reader the claim was checked.

## What to write when you didn't check

- "Not verified: I didn't read the full text, only the abstract."
- "Cannot be verified here: it needs a PCR test or a grow trial."
- "Inferred from X and Y; not measured."

All three beat silence, and all three beat a confident sentence.

## Superseding, not overwriting

When a later finding contradicts an earlier recorded claim, keep the withdrawn wording next to the correction. "This said degeneration causes no growth; the studies found report loss of fruiting only, corrected on this date" teaches the next reader something and shows the question was once open.

This applies only to published or shared claims. A draft can simply be edited.
